\begin{abstract}
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on long chain-of-thought (CoT) traces is a common way to teach small language models to reason,
but it is less clear which traces they should be trained on. We study this question for Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with a budget of
15K examples taken from a 100K subset of AceReason-1.1-SFT. We select the examples with four simple steps that need no labels
or model inference: we keep only math problems, remove duplicate prompts, drop examples that do not fit in the training
context, and sample in proportion across data sources and solution lengths. Trained on the selected data for five epochs, our
final model reaches a macro \passk{8} of 59.0\% on the six leaderboard benchmarks, compared with 53.9\% for the base model and
54.9\% for a model trained on a random 15K subset. It also has the highest macro \passk{1} (26.9\%). Most of the improvement
comes from fewer answers being cut off at the 32,768-token generation limit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A common way to improve the math reasoning of a small model is to fine-tune it on long CoT traces written by a stronger
reasoning model~\citep{openthoughts,acereason11,s1}. This does not always work well for small models. They can struggle to
learn from long traces~\citep{li2025small}, and models trained to reason at length often keep reasoning well past the point
where it helps~\citep{chen2024overthinking}. When the data budget is limited, it therefore matters which examples are used for
training.

In this project~\citep{course}, we fine-tune Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct~\citep{qwen25} on 15K examples taken from a 100K subset of
AceReason-1.1-SFT~\citep{acereason11}, whose responses are long CoT traces generated by DeepSeek-R1. The question is whether
choosing these 15K examples carefully works better than picking them at random. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item A simple selection pipeline that needs no labels or model inference and produces complete, deduplicated math
        demonstrations while keeping the original mix of sources and lengths (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
  \item A final model, trained for five epochs on the selected data, that improves macro \passk{8} by 5.1 points over the base
        model and macro \passk{1} by 2.3 points over the random baseline, with 95\% confidence intervals that exclude zero in
        both cases (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item An analysis showing that the fine-tuned models lose many answers to the generation limit, that fewer such cut-offs
        account for most of our gain, and how much of the gain comes from the data versus the longer training
        (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Data selection strategy}\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Random baseline.} The training pool contains 100,000 examples, of which 67,432 are math and 32,568 are code. The
random baseline samples 15,000 of them uniformly with a fixed seed and no filtering. Of these, 556 are longer than the
16,384-token training context and get truncated, so their final answers never appear in the training target.

\paragraph{Motivation and hypothesis.} A random sample spends part of the budget on examples that are of little use to a 3B
model evaluated on math and science: code traces, repeated questions, and solutions too long for the training context, whose
final answers are cut off. We hypothesize that, with the same 15K budget, training only on complete, in-domain, and
non-redundant demonstrations improves reasoning over a random sample, in particular because the model learns to finish its
answers instead of running past the generation limit.

\paragraph{Selected set.} We apply four steps in order. First, \emph{domain filtering} keeps only the math examples; the
benchmarks are all math or science, so the code traces are of little use. Second, \emph{exact deduplication} removes repeated
prompts (7,730 among the math examples) so that each question appears only once, which increases prompt
diversity~\citep{acereason11}. Third, \emph{length filtering} keeps only examples whose full conversation fits in the
16,384-token context under the Qwen chat template (removing 2,292), so every training target ends with its final answer and
end-of-turn token. Fourth, \emph{stratified sampling} with proportional allocation draws 15,000 of the remaining 57,410
examples across data sources (NuminaMath-CoT and OpenMathReasoning) and length bands (up to 4K, 4--8K, and 8--16K tokens).
This keeps the same mix of sources and of short and long solutions as in the filtered pool, with length serving as a rough
proxy for difficulty. The selected set has 107.6M training tokens per epoch (113.4M for the random set) and a median length of
6.5K tokens, and it shares 2,252 examples with the random set.

\paragraph{Why these steps.} Several studies show that a small, carefully chosen fine-tuning set can match or beat a much
larger unfiltered one, both for alignment~\citep{lima} and for reasoning~\citep{s1,limo}; question filtering and the mix of
data sources also play a central role in recent data recipes for reasoning models~\citep{openthoughts}. Our pipeline needs no
model scoring, embeddings, or answer keys. We also checked for contamination and found six pool prompts that appear verbatim
in a benchmark. One of them ended up in each 15K set; we kept it and report it here rather than removing it from only one of
the two sets.

\section{Experimental setup}

\paragraph{Training.} Both models are trained with full-parameter SFT in LLaMA-Factory~\citep{llamafactory}, using DeepSpeed
ZeRO-3~\citep{zero}, FlashAttention-2~\citep{flashattn2}, and the Liger fused linear cross-entropy kernel~\citep{liger}. The
loss is computed on response tokens only. Table~\ref{tab:hparams} lists the hyperparameters. The final model is first trained
for three epochs; we then resume from its epoch-3 weights, optimizer, and scheduler states and train for two more epochs on a
five-epoch cosine schedule.

\paragraph{Benchmarks and metrics.} We evaluate on AIME24, MATH-500, CN-Math 2024, Kaoyan, AMC, Minerva, OlympiadBench, and
GPQA with the course evaluation harness, which generates answers with vLLM~\citep{vllm} and grades the last boxed answer by
symbolic equivalence. For each question we draw $n=8$ samples with temperature 0.6, top-$p$ 0.95, and at most 32,768 generated
tokens. We report \passk{8}, where a question counts as solved if any of its eight samples is correct (the \passk{k} metric
of \citet{codex} with $k=n$), and \passk{1}, the average accuracy of a single sample. The macro score is the unweighted mean
over the six leaderboard benchmarks we have access to (CN-Math 2024, Kaoyan, AMC, Minerva, OlympiadBench, and GPQA); AIME24 and
MATH-500 are reported but, as in the course leaderboard, not averaged. Differences between models are given in percentage
points with 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) from a paired bootstrap over questions ($B=2000$), which keeps the eight samples of
each question together~\citep{efron1993,miller2024}.

\paragraph{Main results.} Table~\ref{tab:results} shows the scores on all eight benchmarks. Fine-tuning on the random subset
barely changes the base model's macro \passk{8} (+1.1 points, 95\% CI [$-4.2$, 6.2]) and lowers its macro \passk{1} by 1.6
points. The final model does better. It improves macro \passk{8} by 5.1 points over the base model (95\% CI [0.9, 9.5]), the
only gain over the base model that is significant at the 95\% level, and it beats the random baseline by 4.0 points in
\passk{8} (95\% CI [$-0.4$, 8.6]) and by 2.3 points in \passk{1} (95\% CI [0.9, 3.8]). The largest gains are on Kaoyan
(+13.1 points in \passk{8} over the base model) and AMC (+20.0 points over the random baseline). On AIME24, which is not part
of the macro average, it solves three fewer of the 30 problems than the random baseline ($-10.0$ points).

\paragraph{Where the gain comes from.} Both fine-tuned models write reasoning traces that are 8--16$\times$ longer than those
of the base model, in line with the overthinking reported for long-reasoning models~\citep{chen2024overthinking}, and many of
their answers never finish. For the random baseline, 8.7--26.5\% of samples on the leaderboard benchmarks hit the
32,768-token limit before giving an answer (Figure~\ref{fig:cutoff}), and these samples are almost never correct (0--4\%
accuracy). The final model is cut off less often on every leaderboard benchmark (4.8--16.7\%), while its accuracy on answers
that do finish is about the same as that of the random baseline (35.5\% vs.\ 35.2\% on OlympiadBench and 30.9\% vs.\ 29.6\%
on Minerva). Fewer cut-off answers thus lead directly to higher \passk{1} and \passk{8}. Most of this drop comes from training
longer. On a held-out set of 200 pool questions that are in neither training set, the truncation rate of the selected run
falls from 55\% after epoch 1 to 24\% after epoch 3 and 16\% after epoch 5, and \citet{acereason11} similarly find that SFT
accuracy on this data keeps improving up to about five epochs. After three epochs, by contrast, the model trained on the
selected data is cut off about as often as the random baseline: less often on four of the six leaderboard benchmarks and more
often on CN-Math and Minerva.

\paragraph{Data selection or training budget?} The final model was trained for five epochs and the random baseline for three,
so the gap between them mixes the effect of the data with the effect of training longer. At the same three-epoch budget, the
selected data reaches 56.6\% macro \passk{8} and 24.4\% \passk{1}, only 1.6 points above the random baseline in \passk{8}
(95\% CI [$-2.4$, 5.6]), which is within noise. The lead of the final model should therefore be credited to the selected data
and the longer training together, not to data selection alone. Our hypothesis is only partly supported: at the same budget,
the curated data gives a small, non-significant gain and barely changes how often answers are cut off, and the clear
improvement over random sampling appears only when it is combined with longer training.

\paragraph{Limitations.} We trained each configuration once, so our confidence intervals reflect the sampling of benchmark
questions but not the variance across training seeds. We chose the five-epoch schedule after seeing the three-epoch results,
and we picked the final model using the same benchmarks we report on; only AIME25, which is on the course leaderboard but not
available to us, is truly unseen. All of our training data come from the provided pool. The course allows outside data, but it
would first have to be checked for overlap with the benchmarks. Short, curated reasoning sets~\citep{s1,limo}, which may suit
small models better~\citep{li2025small}, and science data for GPQA are natural next steps. Other selection methods we tried,
including a learning-progress selector, are described in the full report in our repository.

\section{Conclusion}

We studied how to choose 15K training examples for reasoning SFT of Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct. Training for five epochs on complete,
deduplicated math demonstrations raises macro \passk{8} from 53.9\% to 59.0\% and macro \passk{1} from 26.2\% to 26.9\%, ahead
of the random baseline (54.9\% and 24.6\%). For a 3B model trained on long CoT data, much of the gain comes from reducing the
number of answers that run past the generation limit, which longer training achieves more than data curation alone. Code, configurations, and evaluation logs are available at
\url{https://github.com/yuan-zhou-git/Course_Project_Trustworthy_ML}, and the final model and datasets at
\url{https://huggingface.co/yuan-zhou-hf}.
